abstract: 'We consider monocular 3D human pose estimation (HPE), focusing on lifting detected 2D keypoints to 3D skeletal joints. Despite their success, we find that commonly used lifting models, including a state-of-the-art probabilistic method, exhibit substantial performance degradation under in-plane rotations.
We systematically study SO(2) rotation equivariance and robustness across lifting models, comparing approaches that learn approximate equivariance via data augmentation with those that enforce exact equivariance via architectural constraints or pose canonicalisation.
We investigate their effects on prediction accuracy, rotational robustness, cross-dataset generalisation, and computational efficiency.
Using established HPE benchmarks, we show that rotation-based augmentation applied jointly to input and output poses substantially improves accuracy on rotated poses, reducing error by up to 72\% with little loss of original-pose accuracy.
These improvements also extend to probabilistic lifting and real-world sequences containing natural full-body rotations.
While augmented models can outperform specialised equivariant architectures at considerably lower inference cost, comparisons using canonicalisation-based versions of the same lifting models demonstrate that exact equivariance need not compromise prediction accuracy.
Direct equivariance diagnostics further show that rotational robustness does not imply exact equivariance.
Overall, the relative benefits of learning or enforcing equivariance depend on the lifting backbone and the mechanism used to achieve it.
Models and code will be published.'

<span style="display:none;"> Learned feature representations in neural networks often contain structure beyond that directly used by the final task output. We study this structure using \emph{passive probes} that apply fixed, untrained, property-independent projections to representations as they evolve during training. We motivate this approach through the task of prediction on $\mathcal{S}^2$ where equivalent vector and Hermitian parameterizations reveal an additional loss-invariant trace coordinate. This motivates a general construction in which fixed random projections serve as observers of learned features.Because the observer is loss-invariant and independent of the property being studied, changes in accessibility reflect changes in the representation relative to the fixed observer rather than adaptation of the observer itself. We show that ensembles of passive probes can directly reflect task-relevant information such as target alignment. Under our constructions, the accessibility of eventual difficulty evolves differently across tasks. It increases during training in the regression tasks of surface-normal estimation and image inpainting but remains near its initial level in image classification. Comparisons with learned linear probes further show that recoverability and passive accessibility can evolve differently during training. Together, these results show how passive probes can separately characterize changes in representation geometry and the accessibility of eventual task difficulty. </span>
